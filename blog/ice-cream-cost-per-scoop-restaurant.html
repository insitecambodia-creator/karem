How Much Does a Scoop of Ice Cream Really Cost a Restaurant?

Understanding portion costs, overheads and profitability when serving ice cream in your restaurant.

Ice cream is one of the simplest and most versatile desserts a restaurant can offer. Whether served on its own, alongside a chocolate fondant or as part of a more elaborate dessert, it requires relatively little preparation and can generate attractive margins.

But how much does a scoop of ice cream actually cost a restaurant once all expenses are considered?

1. The actual cost of a scoop

At Karem, we estimate that a one-litre tub of ice cream or sorbet produces between 10 and 12 scoops, depending on portion size.

2. Taking overheads into consideration

The purchase price is only one component of the actual cost. Restaurant operators should also consider electricity, freezer maintenance, staff time, cleaning, product wastage and a proportion of their general operating expenses.

Let's take the example of a restaurant purchasing Vanilla Ice Cream at $7 per litre, producing an average of 11 scoops.

These figures are illustrative and will naturally vary depending on the restaurant's size, staffing costs, sales volume and operating efficiency.

4. A dessert with minimal preparation and investment

Unlike many traditional desserts, ready-to-serve ice cream does not require a dedicated pastry chef, lengthy preparation or additional cooking equipment.

It offers several advantages for restaurants, hotels and cafés:

  • Minimal preparation and serving time.
  • Easy portion and inventory control.
  • Reduced kitchen workload.
  • A wide variety of flavours without complex preparation.
  • Opportunities to enhance existing desserts and increase their selling price.

A simple scoop of Vanilla Ice Cream can transform a brownie, an apple tart or a warm chocolate cake into a more attractive and valuable dessert.

5. Maximising profitability through portion control

Consistency is key. Using a standardised ice cream scoop and training staff to serve uniform portions can make a significant difference to profitability.

Producing 12 rather than 10 scoops from a one-litre tub reduces the product cost per serving by nearly 17%, provided the portion remains appropriate for the customer and the menu price.

Quality and profitability can go hand in hand

Serving quality ice cream does not have to mean compromising on profitability.
